    Frameworks Level: Practitioner

    Knowledge areas: what to do in each

    The DAM Policy asks for three capabilities: data governance, data cataloguing and data quality. Each rests on work in other data management areas. A catalogue record needs to know where the data lives, who can see it and which system is the authoritative source. A quality plan needs the reference data and the flows that feed it. This guide gives each area the activities that matter for the policy, in the order most organisations need them, down to the steps, outputs and evidence.

    • 10 areas
    • 39 activities
    • As of 2026-11-12

    In brief

    • 39 activities across 10 data management areas, each with steps, outputs, evidence, a measure and who does what.
    • Three stages: what to do before your first return, before your second, and as business as usual.
    • Every activity says which DAM Policy requirements it supports and which standards apply.

    Draft. Draft by this portal for review by the profession that owns each area. Not government guidance. Activities are written fresh by this portal; DAMA-DMBOK is DAMA International's copyrighted work, and only its area names are used here.

    Where to start: three stages

    Pick the activities for the stage you are at. You do not need every activity for your first return.

    1. Start: before your first returnWhat the DAM Policy needs now, so the return is complete and true.
    2. Build: before your second returnMakes the next return easier, and makes critical assets safer to reuse.
    3. Embed: business as usualKeeps the work going without a project, and prepares critical assets for AI and wider sharing.

    Knowledge area · 5 activities

    Data architecture

    The blueprint of data across the organisation: where data lives, how it flows and which source is authoritative.

    Why it matters for the DAM Policy: The policy asks you to designate the source data set behind any distribution you designate (DAM-R12). You can only do that if you know where each critical asset lives, which copy is authoritative and how it flows. Architecture gives the catalogue its structure.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

    Usually led by: Design authority or solution architect. Content owner: Your design authority or architecture community.

    Read with the Data architecture and models supplement.

    • ARC-1 · Start: before your first return

      Map where each critical asset lives

      Goal: One list that says which systems hold each critical asset, so nothing is designated from the wrong copy.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. Start from the candidate and designation log. For each candidate, ask the steward and custodian which systems hold the data.
      2. Record each system, the copy it holds (master, replica, extract, archive) and who runs it.
      3. Mark copies that are spreadsheets, shared drives or personal stores. These are risks, not sources.
      4. Add the landscape to the catalogue record as related resources, not as separate critical assets.
      You produce
      • A data landscape list for critical assets
      • Updated candidate and designation log
      Evidence it is done
      Every designated asset has at least one named system and a named custodian.
      Measure
      Share of critical assets with a named system and custodian.
      Who does what
      • Accountable: Design authority or solution architect
      • Responsible (does the work): Data steward, Catalogue manager
      • Consulted: Data custodian
      • Informed: Data owner
      DAM Policy requirements it supports
      DAM-R01, DAM-R12
      Capabilities it builds
      Identify and scope critical data assets, Data architecture and data services

    • ARC-2 · Start: before your first return

      Name the authoritative source for each critical asset

      Goal: Everyone who reuses the asset knows which copy to trust and where to report problems.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. For each critical asset, decide which system is the authoritative source. If two systems disagree, the data owner decides.
      2. Record the source system, the entities it is authoritative for and how other copies are refreshed from it.
      3. Where you designate a distribution, link it to its source data set in the catalogue (DAM-R12).
      4. Record the decision in the decision log, with the date and who made it.

    • ARC-3 · Build: before your second return

      Draw the main data flows between systems and organisations

      Goal: You can say where critical data goes, so sharing, quality and security risks have a place on the map.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. For each critical asset, list the flows in and out: source, target, method (API, file, manual), frequency and owner.
      2. Mark flows that cross organisational boundaries and link each to its data sharing agreement.
      3. Mark manual re-keying and spreadsheet hand-offs. These are where quality usually drops.
      4. Share the flow list with the data quality lead so quality rules sit where data enters and leaves.
      You produce

      Evidence it is done
      Every external flow of a critical asset has a sharing agreement or a recorded exception.

      Share of external flows with an agreement.

    • ARC-5 · Embed: business as usual

      Make architecture review a gate in change

      Goal: Every change that touches a critical asset updates its landscape, source and flows before it goes live.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      2. If yes, require an updated landscape, flow list and catalogue record before go-live.
      3. Feed spend control and service assessment evidence from the same records rather than a new pack.
      4. Report gate results to the governance board each quarter.

    Data modelling and design

    Defining the structure, meaning and relationships of data.

    Why it matters for the DAM Policy: A catalogue record describes a critical asset; a data model describes what is inside it. Stewards need a model to agree definitions, quality leads need it to write rules, and users need it to judge whether the asset fits their purpose.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

    Usually led by: Design authority or solution architect. Content owner: Your design authority, with data stewards.

    Read with the Data architecture and models supplement.

    • MOD-1 · Start: before your first return

      List the entities and relationships in each critical asset

      Goal: A plain list of what the asset is about (people, cases, payments, places) and how they connect.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. With the steward, name the main things the asset records, in business words.
      2. For each, list its identifier and the few attributes people rely on most.
      3. Record how they relate, for example "a case has one or more payments".
      4. Attach the list to the catalogue record and keep it with the asset, not in a project folder.
      You produce
      • Entity and relationship list
      Evidence it is done
      Each critical asset links to an entity list that its steward has checked.
      Measure
      Share of critical assets with an entity list.
      Who does what
      • Accountable: Data owner
      • Responsible (does the work): Data steward, Design authority or solution architect
      • Consulted: Catalogue manager
      DAM Policy requirements it supports
      DAM-R13, DAM-R02
      Capabilities it builds
      Data architecture and data services, Data cataloguing and metadata management
      Templates
      Entity and relationship list
      Standards and guidance
      • Data Catalog Vocabulary (DCAT) version 3V: Verified against the primary source on 2026-11-05

    • MOD-2 · Build: before your second return

      Agree definitions for the attributes people rely on

      Goal: The same word means the same thing in every system and report that uses the asset.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. Pick the attributes that drive decisions, payments or published figures.
      2. Write one plain-English definition for each and name the steward who owns it.
      3. Check the definition against cross-government standards and the business glossary before writing a new one.
      4. Link each attribute to its glossary term, so a change to the definition reaches every model that uses it.
      You produce
      • Attribute definitions linked to glossary terms
      Evidence it is done
      Key attributes link to an approved glossary term with a named owner.
      Measure
      Share of key attributes linked to an approved term.
      Who does what

      • Consulted: Catalogue manager, Data quality lead or analyst

    • MOD-3 · Build: before your second return

      Publish a data dictionary with the catalogue record

      Goal: Users can see the columns, types and allowed values before they ask for access.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. Export the column names, types, formats and allowed values from the authoritative source.
      2. Add the definitions from MOD-2 and mark personal or sensitive fields.
      3. Publish the dictionary as a machine-readable file (for example CSV on the Web) linked from the catalogue record.
      4. Regenerate it from the source when the structure changes, rather than editing by hand.

    • MOD-4 · Embed: business as usual

      Control changes to models

      Goal: A change to structure never breaks reports, sharing or quality rules without warning.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. Version each model and dictionary. Record what changed and why.
      2. Before a change, list who uses the affected attributes, using the flow list and reuse records.
      3. Tell users in advance and give a date. Mark attributes as retired rather than deleting them.
      4. Where others depend on the asset, record the new structure in its data contract (see INT-4).
      You produce
      • Versioned models
      • Change notices
      • Data contracts for shared assets
      Evidence it is done
      Structure changes have a version, a notice and a date.
      Measure
      Unplanned breaks to downstream users each year.

      • Accountable: Data steward

      • Consulted: Data quality lead or analyst
      • Informed: Data users

    Data storage and operations

    Running the platforms that store data: backup, recovery, performance and retention.

    Why it matters for the DAM Policy: A critical asset is only useful if it is available, recoverable and kept for the right time. The policy's purposes, such as public service delivery, depend on the data being there when needed.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

    Usually led by: Data custodian. Content owner: Your technology operations or hosting team.

    • OPS-1 · Start: before your first return

      Name the custodian and hosting for each critical asset

      Goal: Someone is responsible for keeping each asset running, and you know where it is hosted.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. Add a custodian to each ownership register entry, separate from the data owner.
      2. Record the hosting (on premises, cloud, supplier service) and the contract that covers it.
      3. Record where the data is physically stored, if your security or contracts require it.
      4. Tell the custodian which assets are critical and why.
      You produce
      • Custodian and hosting in the ownership register
      Evidence it is done
      Every critical asset has a custodian distinct from its owner, or a recorded reason why not.
      Measure
      Share of critical assets with a named custodian.
      Who does what
      • Accountable: Data owner
      • Responsible (does the work): Data custodian
      • Consulted: Data steward
      • Informed: Lead policy coordinator
      DAM Policy requirements it supports
      DAM-R05, DAM-R02

      Data ownership and stewardship, Data architecture and data services

    • OPS-2 · Build: before your second return

      Back up critical assets and test that you can restore them

      Goal: A failure or attack does not lose critical data or the service that depends on it.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. Agree with the owner how much data the service could lose and how long it could be without it.
      2. Check backups meet that, including copies held offline or out of reach of ransomware.
      3. Test a restore at least once a year and record the result.
      4. Fix gaps and record any accepted risk in the exception register.
      You produce

      Evidence it is done
      A dated restore test for each critical asset.
      Measure
      Share of critical assets restored in a test in the last 12 months.

      • Consulted: Service or product owner, Information asset owner

      Monitor availability, freshness and capacity

      Goal: Problems are found by the custodian before users find them.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. Monitor whether each critical asset is available and when it was last updated.
      2. Set a freshness threshold with the data quality lead and alert when it is missed.
      3. Feed failures into the data quality issue log, not just the service desk.
      4. Review capacity before peaks, such as year end.

    • OPS-4 · Embed: business as usual

      Plan retention, archiving and decommissioning

      Goal: Data is kept as long as needed and no longer, and nothing critical is lost when a system retires.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      2. Archive or delete copies on schedule and record what was done.
      3. When a system retires, migrate or archive critical data first, and update the catalogue record.
      4. Remove decommissioned copies so they cannot be used by mistake.

    Data security

    Protecting data from unauthorised access, change and loss, in line with classification.

    Why it matters for the DAM Policy: In-scope assets are classified OFFICIAL, including handling markings such as 'personal' (DAM-R01). The catalogue must say how each asset is protected and who may use it, or it cannot be shared safely.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

    Usually led by: Information asset owner. Content owner: Your security team, data protection officer and information asset owners.

    • SEC-1 · Start: before your first return

      Record classification and personal data for each asset

      Goal: Every catalogue record says how the asset is classified and whether it holds personal data.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. Record the classification and any handling marking from your information asset register.
      2. Mark whether the asset holds personal or special category data, with the data protection officer.
      3. Link to the data protection impact assessment where there is one.
      4. Check the record before it goes into the return. Never put the data itself in the catalogue.
      You produce
      • Classification and personal data fields in each record
      Evidence it is done
      No critical asset record has a blank classification.
      Measure
      Share of records with classification and personal data recorded.
      Who does what
      • Accountable: Information asset owner
      • Responsible (does the work): Data protection officer, Catalogue manager
      • Consulted: Data steward

      Goal: Each important term has one agreed meaning, an owner and a history.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. For each term, record a preferred label, other labels (synonyms and abbreviations), a one-sentence plain-English definition and a scope note saying when to use it.
      2. Name the owner (a steward), the source standard or law it comes from, and the body that endorsed it.
      3. Give it a status (draft, in review, approved, deprecated), a version and valid from and to dates.
      4. Map it to the equivalent term in other standards. When a term retires, point to its replacement; terms are deprecated, never deleted.
      5. Review each term every year, or when its source changes, and log every change.
      You produce
      • A business glossary with owners
      • status and history
      Evidence it is done
      Approved terms have an owner, a source and a review date.

    • META-2 · Embed: business as usual

      Climb from glossary to knowledge graph

      Goal: Your terms, standards, assets and owners become linked data that people, catalogues and AI tools can query.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
      1. Level 1, glossary: agreed terms with owners (META-1).
      2. Level 2, thesaurus: relate terms as broader, narrower, related, and "use instead", so search finds the preferred term.
      3. Level 3, vocabularies and taxonomies: closed code lists and a classification tree (category, topic, subject) applied the same way everywhere.
      4. Level 4, ontologies: describe concepts with SKOS, data sets with DCAT, lineage with PROV-O and quality with DQV, and validate them with SHACL shapes before publishing.
      5. Level 5, knowledge graph: give each term, standard, asset and owner a persistent, resolvable identifier and publish the links, so questions such as "who owns this term?" and "what breaks if it changes?" can be answered by query.

      • Machine-readable vocabularies with persistent identifiers

      Make critical assets ready for AI

      Goal: Critical assets can be used by AI tools safely, with known quality, meaning and limits.O: This playbook's own synthesis of published guidance

      Steps
